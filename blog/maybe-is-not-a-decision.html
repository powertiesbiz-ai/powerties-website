Maybe Is Not a Decision

By Keith Tully, Founder, PowerTies Consulting Group

By Keith Tully, PowerTies Consulting Group

The most expensive sentence in business is "Let me think about it."

Nobody who says it is lying. They really are going to think about it, the way we all think about the gym membership in January. But here is what actually happened in that moment: no decision was made, no information was exchanged, and both people walked away believing they had communicated. They had not. They had scheduled a fog.

We have a whole dictionary of these phrases, and everybody speaks it fluently:

  • "I'll call you back." Translation: I do not want to say no to your face, so I am outsourcing it to silence.
  • "We should definitely get together sometime." That is not a plan. That is a greeting wearing a plan's coat.
  • "Let's circle back on this." The meeting produced nothing, and everyone at the table is too polite to say so.
  • "We're very interested." Interest is not a decision. It is a weather report.
  • "I just need some time to think." Sometimes true. Often a goodbye that did not want to pay for a stamp.

Relationships speak the same dialect, and there it does real damage. "I think we need some time apart." "I just don't feel like I know who I am right now." Most of the time, one person has already decided and does not want to be the one who said it out loud. So instead of one honest, painful conversation, both people move into a hallway and live there. Weeks pass. Months. Nobody is together, nobody is free, and nobody is allowed to start healing, because technically nothing has been decided. Limbo is the worst outcome on the table, and hedging is how people choose it while telling themselves they chose kindness.

A soft answer is not a favor

People hedge because they believe a hard answer hurts and a soft one helps. It is exactly backwards. A clean no takes ten seconds. A polite maybe costs the other person three weeks of planning around a door that was never going to open.

In business, that means the salesperson keeps forecasting a deal that died in the first meeting. The owner keeps holding a slot, a crew, a price. In a relationship, it means somebody keeps their whole life on pause, checking their phone, waiting for a decision that was made without them.

Respecting a person's time is one of the most loving things you can do. A real no respects it. A fog wastes it. If we actually care about people, we owe them answers they can build on.

How to ask for a real answer, with love

The fix is not being blunt, and it is not cornering anyone. It is two skills: ask the hard question out loud, and make "no" safe to say.

In sales, when you hear "I have to think about it," do not argue and do not slink away. Agree, and then build the bridge: "Good. Thinking is part of doing this right. Let's put twenty minutes on the calendar Thursday to talk it through. And when we talk, if the answer is no, that is a perfectly good answer. I would rather have your honest no on Thursday than a polite maybe that drags us both into next month." Book the follow-up before the call ends. A think-about-it without a date attached is not a process. It is an exit.

Then, on that follow-up, ask the question the whole conversation has been avoiding: "Are you ready to move forward? And if not, what is actually holding you up?" Ask it warmly, and mean it. Whatever is holding them up is the real conversation. Price, timing, a spouse, a fear they have not said out loud: that is where the truth lives, and you cannot help somebody with an obstacle they have not been invited to name.

In a relationship, the same principle applies, with more heart and the same backbone. If someone says "I need time apart," you can honor that and still ask for shape: "I can respect that. Help me understand what we are deciding, and by when. Are we working on this, or are we ending it kindly? I can live with either answer. I cannot live in the hallway." That is not pressure. That is refusing to let two people bleed out slowly from a wound a single honest sentence could close.

Be the person who gives clean answers, too

Deliberate communication runs both directions. Say yes like you mean it, and no like you respect the person. Replace "I'll get back to you" with "I will tell you by Friday," and then tell them by Friday, even if the answer is no. People learn fast that your word is load-bearing, and a strange thing happens: they start giving you straight answers back. Clarity is contagious.

None of this is harsh. Harsh is letting someone plan around a maybe. Harsh is keeping a person in limbo because saying the true thing feels awkward for an afternoon. Deliberate is kind. Deliberate is how you hand people their time back.

So stop the fog. Ask the hard question, make no a safe answer, and give the same honesty you are asking for. Yes is a gift. No is a gift. Maybe is just a bill somebody else has to pay.
