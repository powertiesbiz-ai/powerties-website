What Is an AI Readiness Assessment (and Do You Need One)?

By Keith Tully, Founder, PowerTies Consulting Group

An owner I spoke with had three AI tools on his credit card and none of them in his daily work. He had bought them in a burst of energy, set each one up halfway, and gone back to doing everything by hand. His problem was not the tools. He had never stopped to check where his business actually stood before he bought anything.

That check is what an AI readiness assessment is. As the founder of PowerTies Consulting Group, I mean it in a plain sense. A short, honest look at your business before you spend money on AI, so the first thing you try is the thing most likely to pay off.

What an assessment is, and what it is not

An assessment is not an exam. There is no passing grade. It is not a sales pitch, and it should not end with a list of ten tools to buy.

Think of it the way a good mechanic looks at a car before quoting a repair. Check the basics first, then fix what is actually costing you. At the end you should have a short ranked list. Do this first. This can wait. Skip this entirely.

The first thing it looks at: where your time goes

Every readiness check starts with time drains. These are the repeating tasks that eat your week in small pieces.

Writing the same email again. Turning rough notes into a quote. Chasing a customer who has not replied. Summarizing a call so the next step is clear. None of these feel large on their own. Together, they eat the week.

The assessment asks a simple question. Which tasks repeat every week, follow roughly the same pattern, and do not need your personal judgment at every step? Those are where AI can help first, because AI is good at first drafts, summaries, and reminders. It is not good at judgment calls.

The second thing: the information your business runs on

AI works from what you give it. If your prices live in your head, your services are described three different ways, and your customer notes are scattered across texts and paper, any tool you buy will give you generic work back. Generic work is what owners abandon.

So an assessment asks, in plain terms. Do you have a current price list a tool could use? A clear list of services, in words a customer understands? A place where job notes actually get written down?

You do not need a fancy system. A single clean document with your prices and services is enough to start. The point is to learn whether the raw material is ready, or whether a short cleanup comes first.

The third thing: team habits

Tools do not stick on their own. People use them, or they do not.

An assessment looks at how your team already works. Who would use the new tool each day? Does that person have time to learn it, and a reason to care? Has the business dropped tools before, and if so, why?

The larger companies that get real value from AI did not just buy software. They picked one workflow, trained people on it, and stuck with it until it became habit. A small business can do the same at its own size. Buying a fourth tool will not fix a habit that broke the first three.

The fourth thing: the first win test

The last part is the most important. A good assessment ends by picking a first win and defining it in advance.

A first win is small, specific, and measurable in hours. One task, one tool, thirty days. Then judge it by one question. How many hours did it hand back, with the work still done well?

If yes, try the second task. If no, you learned something cheap, and you stop instead of renewing another subscription out of hope.

Do you need one?

You probably do if any of these sound familiar. You have tried tools and nothing stuck. You use AI for your own writing but the business does not run on it. Everything still runs on manual effort and memory. Or you do not know where to start, and every article points in a different direction.

You can skip it for now if one repeating task is already handled well by one tool, your team uses it without being chased, and your next step is obvious. Keep going and check again in a few months.

Most owners are in the first group. That is not a failure. It is the normal starting line.
