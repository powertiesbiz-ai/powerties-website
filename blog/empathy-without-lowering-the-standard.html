Not Broken: Meeting People Where They Are Without Lowering the Standard

By Keith Tully, Founder, PowerTies Consulting Group

Watch what happens when a good leader meets someone whose way of working makes no sense to him. Within a few months, often without noticing it, he starts trying to renovate the person. A little more structure here. A little less of that habit there. Soften this edge, sand down that one. The intention is usually decent. The assumption underneath it is not. The assumption is that different means damaged.

Sometimes a person does need correction. We will get to that, because the standard matters in this piece as much as the person does. But a great deal of what leaders call fixing is something else. It is taking a person who was formed somewhere we have never been, and trying to make them normal by our lights. And normal, most of the time, just means "like the people already in this room."

Tarzan was not broken

Think about Tarzan. A boy raised in the jungle, in an environment almost nobody in our world understands. In that jungle he is not a problem to be solved. He is the most capable creature there. He reads signs in the dirt that other men cannot even see. He hears what is coming long before it arrives. He moves through his world with a strength and a sense that look, to anyone watching from outside, almost like super powers.

Now put that same man in a London drawing room. He does not know the customs. He does not speak the way they speak or sit the way they sit. In that room, he looks broken. He looks weird.

Nothing about the man changed. The room changed. His upbringing, the very thing the drawing room cannot understand, is what built the abilities the drawing room cannot match. Weird is what genius looks like from a room that cannot read where a person came from.

Every person you have ever called weird was raised in some jungle you have not visited. A house. A neighborhood. A trade. A hardship. A faith. A family that ran on rules completely different from yours. That environment built abilities into them that your environment never built into you. It also built blind spots, because every environment does. But the first thing their difference tells you is not that they are broken. It tells you where to look.

Scripture said it first

The Bible told this story long before we had a word for it. When David went out to face Goliath, King Saul did the respectable thing. He dressed the boy in the king's own armor. It was the best equipment in the kingdom, and it was completely wrong for the person wearing it. David could barely walk in it. His words were simple: I cannot go with these, for I have not tested them. So he took the armor off, picked up his staff and his sling, chose five smooth stones from the brook, and went out to meet the giant with the tools his shepherd years had built into his hands.

Notice what did not change. The standard did not change. Goliath still had to fall. What changed was the assumption that David had to become someone else, dressed in someone else's strength, before he was allowed to fight. God had spent years training that boy in a field nobody was watching, against lions and bears nobody else saw. The jungle was the training ground. The sling was the genius.

It is the same pattern when God meets Moses at the burning bush and Moses is full of reasons he is the wrong man. God does not hand him new equipment. He asks a question: "What is that in your hand?" A staff. An ordinary shepherd's tool, already in his grip. And Paul makes the principle impossible to miss when he describes the church as a body. The eye cannot say to the hand, "I have no need of you." The members that seem weaker are the ones he calls indispensable, and he says plainly that God arranged the members in the body as He chose. Different gifts. Different functions. One body, and no member gets to call another one a mistake.

Weird is a signal, not a verdict

That is the discipline in this, and it is a discipline. Difference is data. It is a signal to pay attention, not a verdict in either direction.

It does not mean everything different is good. It does not mean every odd way of working is a hidden gift and every correction is oppression. Some differences harm people. Some break trust. Some are just ways of dodging the work. Those are not genius, and no amount of empathy renames them.

So here is the test I use. Genius is the difference that produces something of real value when it is placed right. The standard is the filter that tells the difference between a strength in the wrong seat and a problem that has to be addressed. Meeting people where they are never means lowering where we are going. It means being honest about where they are standing before we ask them to move.

This is also where two very ordinary sentences do more damage than we know. "I don't like the way you do that." And "that's not for me." Said once, they are opinions about a method. Said by the same person, about everything a person does, over and over, they stop sounding like opinions. They start sounding like a verdict on the person. And very often, if we are honest, what those sentences actually mean is smaller than we admit. I just cannot see it.

So before you speak, ask the question that sorts the whole thing out. Is this wrong, or is it just not mine? If it is wrong, if it breaks the standard, say so plainly and say why. If it is just not yours, that is a different conversation entirely, and it usually starts with curiosity instead of correction.

Where the standard comes from

Everything in this piece leans on one phrase: real value. So we have to settle where the standard comes from, because the standard is what decides what real value is. Real value is not what impresses people. It is not what is normal in somebody else's room. Real value is whatever pushes the mission forward, and the standard is how a person or an organization says so, on purpose, before the pressure is on.

A standard is not found, and it is not borrowed. It is built. You build it out of the values and goals you have decided on, for yourself or for the organization you are part of. The build order matters. Values come first: they say who we are. Then the mission and the goals: they say where we are going and what we are here to do. Then the standard, which is simply the line those two draw across the daily work. Given who we are and where we are going, this is how we work, and this is what counts.

That is why a borrowed standard never fits. Saul's armor was a fine standard, for Saul. It was built out of his size, his training, his way of fighting. On David it was not a standard at all. It was a costume. A good share of the standards hanging on the walls of businesses are costumes like that, copied from a bigger company, a book, or a previous job, and never traced back to the values and the mission of the people actually wearing them. When a standard cannot be traced back up the chain to a value and the mission, it is not a standard. It is a preference, a habit, or somebody else's armor, and it deserves to be treated that way.

A real standard also has to fit four things at once: the individual, the team, the culture, and the mission. If it fits the mission but crushes the individual's genius, you may have a placement problem, or a standard that was written for a different kind of person. If it fits the people comfortably but does not push the mission forward, it is not a standard either. It is just comfort with a title. The four fits are how you test a standard before you enforce it, and how you tell a bad standard apart from a good person standing in the wrong seat.

And a standard exists to do a job. It pushes the mission forward, and it helps the work get through the obstacles in front of it. A standard that only polices people and never moves the mission is a fence. A standard that serves the mission is a tool, and it earns its place every time the road gets hard.

The last piece is the one that keeps standards from becoming a cage: standards yes, expectations no. A standard describes faithfulness. An expectation quietly becomes a ceiling. This much, from this person, in this way, by this time. The moment expectations take over, we stop seeing anything beyond them, including the genius we were supposed to be looking for. So hold the standard firmly and hold the outcome open. The standard said Goliath falls. It never said he falls by a sword, in armor, on Saul's schedule. The master in the parable of the talents judged faithfulness, not sameness: five talents became five more, two became two more, different men and different amounts, and the standard was met by both. Expectations would have graded them against each other. The standard graded each man against what was in his hand.

So here is the working test for any standard, in one sentence. Does it push the mission forward and help us through the obstacle in front of us, without shrinking the people doing the work? If yes, it is a standard, and you uphold it without apology. If no, you rebuild it, no matter how long it has been on the wall.

What empathy does not mean

Let me be plain about the boundaries, because this idea gets soft fast if you let it.

Does meeting people where they are mean agreeing with them when you do not agree? No.

Does it mean changing everything you do so nobody is ever uncomfortable? No.

Does it mean slacking on standards? Absolutely not. A standard you will not state is not a standard. It is a hope. And a leader who lowers the bar so everybody stays comfortable has not been kind to anybody. He has just made the whole group smaller, including the very people he was trying to protect.

Empathy is not softness. Empathy is accuracy. It is knowing what your words carry when they land, and knowing what a person's history built into them before you decide what needs fixing.

Modern work keeps proving the same point

Roger James Hamilton, who founded GeniusU, built his whole platform on one sentence he puts at the top of his Genius Test: "Everyone is a genius. But if you judge a fish by its ability to climb a tree, it will live its whole life believing that it is stupid." His research with entrepreneurs sorts genius into four natural kinds. Dynamo genius creates, always asking what could exist. Blaze genius connects, working through people. Tempo genius senses timing and serves the right person at the right moment. Steel genius masters the details and the systems. His central claim is that your genius is natural to you, the way your writing hand is natural, and that much of our frustration comes from taking good advice that was meant for a different genius. A Dynamo suffocating in a Steel job is not broken. He is a fish being graded on tree climbing.

The practice of NLP, neuro-linguistic programming, starts from nearly the same place, and it says so in so many words. One of its founding presuppositions is that you meet people in their own unique model of the world. Another is that the map is not the territory: your picture of a person is not the person. And the one I quote most is that the resources a person needs to make a change are already within them. NLP describes itself as a generative model rather than a repair model. Read that again. A whole modern discipline of human change looked at the repair kit and set it down. The coach's job is not to fix a broken person. It is to help a whole person find and use what is already in their hand.

Baseball learned it in public too. In the early 2000s the Oakland Athletics had one of the smallest payrolls in the game, so they started measuring players by a standard the rich teams were ignoring, whether the man could get on base. Suddenly players the whole league had written off as flawed, odd bodies, strange swings, wrong everything, turned out to be full of value nobody had priced. Oakland won twenty straight games in 2002 with those players. The players did not change. The looking changed.

Three different worlds, one conclusion. Scripture, a modern genius platform, a school of communication, and a baseball front office all keep finding the same thing. The person is rarely the problem. The measuring is.

Everyone has a genius, and finding it is the leader's job

That is the leader's real work with people. Not repair first. Search first.

It is why I built the 360 Personal Value Review the way I did. It does not begin by asking what is wrong with a person. It begins with twenty-five reflective questions about what a person has actually done, where they have succeeded, what they carried, what they built, what they survived. Then it assembles that record into a clear statement of their personal value, the strengths narrative that was sitting in their own history the whole time. I have watched people who were called weird for years read their own record and sit up straighter. The genius was never missing. Nobody had asked the right questions.

Every person on your team has some version of that record. Your job is to find where their jungle made them strong, and then put that strength in a seat where it produces value. That is not lowering the standard. That is how the standard gets met by people who could never have met it your way.

A talented leader's best work

All of this points at what I believe is a talented leader's best work: helping people see how to be part of something greater than themselves.

People do not give their best to a task. They give their best to a purpose they can see themselves inside of. And part of that picture is showing people how they get what they want by doing certain things. Not manipulation. Alignment. When a person can see that the standard you are holding is not a wall in front of what they want, but the road to it, everything changes. They stop resisting the standard and start using it.

That is helping others not in spite of yourself, but in collaboration with yourself. You do not have to shrink to make room for people. You do not have to disappear, or pretend to agree, or sand down everything you believe. You bring yourself, your standards, your way of seeing it, and you put all of that in service of helping other people rise, starting from where they actually stand.

Hold both

So hold both. Hold the standard exactly where it is, and hold off on the repair kit until you have looked for the genius. Refuse what is wrong, plainly. And when a person is simply not yours, formed in a jungle you have not visited, remember that weird is a signal. It is the beginning of a search, not the end of one.

As founder and CEO of PowerTies Consulting Group, I work with owners and leaders who rarely need lower standards. What they need, and what I need, is to stop trying to fix people who are not broken, and start finding the strength their lives already built into them.
