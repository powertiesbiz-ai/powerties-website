You Get More Than One Shot

By Keith Tully, Founder, PowerTies Consulting Group

I love music. The best songs evoke an emotion that transcends the thinking mind and hits hard somewhere in your soul. They do not argue with you. They just land.

The other day I was listening to the radio, and "Lose Yourself" by Eminem came on. It is one of those songs. Instantly recognizable from the first notes, the anthem of the movie 8 Mile, and one of the songs that transported Eminem to great heights in his career.

But listen to the famous line in it. You only get one shot. This opportunity comes once in a lifetime. It is a great line. It is also, in one way, contradicted by the life of the man who wrote it.

Before there was ever a shot for Eminem to take, he spent years failing in public. An early album that went nowhere. Battles he lost. Rooms that did not want him there. If opportunity really came once, his story would have ended in those years, long before anyone knew his name. Instead, the shot everyone remembers showed up because he kept standing in the room, getting better, long after most people would have gone home.

I do not think he would even argue the point. Read the lyric closely and the real subject is not the number of chances. It is readiness. Lose yourself in the moment only works if you did the unglamorous work before the moment arrived. He did. That is the part of the song worth keeping.

Like all great art, a song does not stay in one place. The lyrics hit differently at different parts of our lives. Faith works the same way. You can hear the same words, read the same passage, for years, and then one morning they land completely differently, because you are in a different place than you were the last time you heard them.

That is what happened to me with this song. I have been listening to it for years. The contradiction never hit me as plainly as it did this morning. The song did not change. I did. I was finally in a place to hear what was actually in it.

Another song on the radio

A few years ago Panic! At The Disco had a hit called "High Hopes." The singer says he did not have a dime, but he always had a vision. He always had high hopes. He always knew he was going to be that one in a million.

Put the two songs side by side and they sound like an argument. One says you get a single chance and it is gone forever. The other says hold the vision and keep going. They are not actually arguing. They are describing two halves of the same life.

"Lose Yourself" is about the moment. "High Hopes" is about everything before and after the moment. The moment matters. It just is not the whole story, and it is never the only moment you get.

Why the one-shot idea does real damage

Believing in one shot sounds motivating. In practice it does two harmful things.

First, it makes people freeze. If this is the only chance you will ever get, the pressure feels total, and total pressure makes most people smaller, not sharper.

Second, it makes people quit. Miss the shot and the story is supposedly over. I talk with business owners who live this out. One lost contract, one bad hire, one launch that flopped, and they start speaking about the business in the past tense. Meanwhile the phone still rings. The next opportunity is already walking toward them and they cannot see it, because they decided opportunity was a thing that happened once.

A missed opportunity is a lesson. It is only a verdict if you stop there.

Nobody learns anything in one try

School trained most of us backwards. Get 100 on the test. Avoid the red marks. Perfection was the assignment, and failure was treated like a character flaw.

Real skills do not work that way. Nobody learns to walk by standing up one day and walking. You learn by falling, hundreds of times, until falling teaches your body what balance is. Same with riding a bike. Same with lifting weights. Strength is built across months of sessions, most of them unremarkable.

Leadership works the same way. Nobody becomes a good leader in a day. You handle a situation badly. You handle the next one a little better. You think about what happened, and the thinking becomes part of you. Years later someone calls you a natural leader, and it looks like it appeared overnight. It did not. It accumulated.

Business is no different. The overnight success is the most durable myth in it. Look under any of them and you find years of preparation that nobody photographed. And even in the rare case where success really did arrive fast, three things still had to be true at the same time. The person recognized the opportunity as an opportunity. They had the means to act on it. And they had built enough skill to turn a moment into something that lasted. Recognition, means, and skill are all built in advance, during the years when it looks like nothing is happening.

What to do with this

Opportunity is not a once-in-a-lifetime event. It is a repeating one. It appears to come to prepared people more than talented ones because preparation is what makes an opportunity visible in the first place. The same week can look empty to one owner and full of openings to another. The difference is rarely luck. It is readiness. And readiness is not something that happens to you. We build our own readiness for the opportunities that come our way.

So the practical questions change. Not "what if I miss my one shot," but these:

What am I building now that a future opportunity would need? What did the last miss teach me, in specific terms, not general regret? If a good opportunity arrived next month, would I recognize it, and could I act on it?

This is also where another old saying falls apart: ignorance is bliss. Not knowing an opportunity exists does not protect anybody. A person can be miserable in ignorance and not even know why. The opportunity was there. They just could not see it, and the not seeing has a cost whether they feel it or not.

Seeing clearly takes real work. It takes work to see your life's purpose clearly, and more work to dedicate yourself to that purpose wholly, through the ups and the downs, and straight through the obstacles. And sometimes the deepest shift is learning to see the obstacles themselves differently. An obstacle can be an opportunity in its own right. An opportunity to practice patience. An opportunity to build resilience. The thing standing in your way becomes part of the training.

As founder and CEO of PowerTies Consulting Group, I spend my days with owners who think their chance passed them by. It almost never has. There is another one coming. The only real question is what you will be ready for when it gets here.
