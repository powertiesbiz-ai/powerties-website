Peaceful Trust: Believing You Are Loved

By Keith Tully, Founder, PowerTies Consulting Group

My wife Maria makes me feel loved every day. She does it in small, ordinary ways, the kind that do not look like much from the outside. Consideration. Reliability. A hundred little moments that say, without making a speech about it, you matter to me. I start with that fact because everything in this article stands on it, and because I know how many people cannot say the same sentence, even when the love around them is just as real.

My name is Keith Tully. Most of my work is business. I help owners build companies that run on standards and systems instead of stress. But this piece is about something underneath all of that, because I keep meeting people, capable people, loved people, who are quietly not sure they are loved at all. And I have come to believe the problem is almost never the love. The problem is the filter it has to pass through to land.

The love is already there

Start with the fact, not the feeling. Love is being shown to you every day, in the ordinary run of your life. A partner's consideration and reliability. A friend who checks in and shows up. Family whose care has run so long it looks like furniture. And underneath all of it, before any of them knew your name, God, who loved you first. Scripture puts it in one line: "We love because he first loved us" (1 John 4:19).

So the question is not "Am I loved?" That one is settled. The question is "Why do I sometimes not believe it, and what does my mindset have to do with that?"

Pope St. John Paul II opened his first encyclical, Redemptor Hominis, with the claim that man cannot live without love. If love is not revealed to him and he does not encounter it, his life remains senseless to him. Being loved, and knowing it, is not a luxury for soft moments. It is the ground a life stands on.

Being loved and believing you are loved are two different things. The first is about the people around you and about God. The second is about your filter. You cannot control the first. You can train the second.

The filter decides what lands

This is not just a nice idea. Some of the best-known researchers in psychology have spent careers measuring exactly how the mind filters love before it ever reaches the heart.

Start with the negativity bias. Psychologist Roy Baumeister and his colleagues reviewed hundreds of studies and summed them up in the title of their paper: "Bad Is Stronger Than Good." The brain gives bad moments more weight than good ones of the same size. One cold exchange can erase ten kind ones, not because the kind ones were not real, but because the brain is built to guard against threat first. If you do not know this filter is running, you will mistake it for the truth about your relationships.

Then there is what Dr. John Gottman calls sentiment override. Gottman has studied couples for over forty years, and he describes two settings a close relationship can run on. In positive sentiment override, a rough moment gets read generously: she is tired, he is stressed, it is not about me. In negative sentiment override, even a kind or neutral gesture gets read through suspicion. Same person, same gesture, opposite meaning. The setting, not the gesture, decides what you believe.

Gottman also found that love is usually offered in tiny moments he calls bids for connection: a comment, a touch, a question, a look, a text that says "thinking of you." In his studies of married couples, partners who stayed happily together turned toward those bids about 86 percent of the time. Couples who later divorced turned toward them about 33 percent of the time. Friends and family make bids too, in their own ways. Love is mostly small. A mindset waiting for grand gestures will walk past a day full of love and report that nothing happened.

There is an old saying in the Church about windows and sunlight, and it fits here. The filter is not the weather report. It is the window. A dirty window does not prove the sun is not shining.

What peaceful trust is

Peaceful trust is the settled confidence that you are loved, by God first and without condition, and by the people in your life, held deeply enough that it does not rise and fall with circumstances.

Read that slowly. The confidence is settled, not re-argued every morning. The love is received, not performed for. And the circumstances, good or bad, do not get to vote on whether it is true.

Peaceful trust is a stance, not a feeling. Feelings move with sleep, weather, blood sugar, and the last hard conversation. A stance is something you take on purpose and return to on purpose. It is clear-eyed, not denial. It does not pretend the storm is not real. It refuses to let the storm decide what is true.

It is also how a person remains calm in the midst of chaos. Chaos is not impressed by your plans. The diagnosis comes. The deal falls apart. A child is struggling. A key employee quits at the worst hour. A person without an anchor reads the storm as a verdict on his worth and his future, and he makes his decisions from panic. A person with peaceful trust reads the same storm as weather: real, sometimes dangerous, but not the deepest truth in the room. He can be calm because something in him is not up for negotiation.

And peaceful trust sits at the center of two things at once. It is the center of a healthy love of yourself, because a person at peace about being loved does not spend his strength earning his worth, so he has strength left for his work, his family, and his people. And it is the center of love for others, because a person who trusts he is loved can give without grasping, help without keeping a receipt, and stay steady when the people around him are not.

Love speaks in dialects

Part of missing love is looking for it in only one shape. The Catechism, quoting St. Thomas Aquinas, gives the cleanest definition ever written: "To love is to will the good of the other" (CCC 1766). Every way people show love is just that one sentence wearing different clothes. Someone willing your good, and showing it the way they know how.

Gary Chapman, a counselor who wrote The 5 Love Languages after years of marriage counseling, made the same point in practical terms. People give and receive love in different languages: words of affirmation, quality time, acts of service, gifts, and physical touch. Each person has a primary one, the dialect that lands deepest. His picture is an emotional love tank in each of us. Speak a person's primary language and the tank fills. Speak only your own and the tank runs dry, even while love is being offered daily.

The failure Chapman kept meeting in his counseling office is the exact failure this article is about. Two people, both loving, neither feeling loved, because the husband was speaking acts of service to a wife whose language was time, or the friend was giving gifts to someone who only trusts presence. Nothing was missing except translation.

So before you conclude that someone does not love you, translate. A partner may speak mostly in service: the fixed sink was the love letter. A friend may speak in presence: he does not say much, but he is always there when it counts. A parent may speak in provision and sacrifice, years of both, long before they ever learned to say the words. If you only count one dialect, you will undercount every relationship you have.

Five shifts that open the filter

The research and the practice point to five shifts. Each one is small. Together they change what a day looks like.

Growth, not fixed. Connection is a skill you build, not a verdict you receive. A rough week is a season, not a sentence. People who treat love as something that grows get better at receiving it, because they stop grading every day like a final exam.

Curiosity, not defensiveness. Ask what they mean before you decide what they meant. A question costs little. A wrong assumption can cost a whole evening, or a whole friendship.

Self-regulation, not scorekeeping. Own your mood, and stop asking love to prove itself daily. Scorekeeping turns care into a ledger, every gesture gets audited, and no one can love well under audit. The trap to watch is demanding proof. The moment love has to prove itself, every proof gets cross-examined and none of them count.

Intentional attention. Train your eyes on the small daily evidence of love. Your brain filters for whatever you aim it at. Deliberately noticing acts of consideration, reliability, and effort is a practice, and the practice changes what you see.

Clear communication. Say how you receive love, and learn how each person gives it. Half of receiving love well is learning the other person's dialect. The other half is teaching them yours, out loud, kindly, and more than once.

The root: a healthy love of yourself

Underneath all of it sits a root. How well you know you are loved, and how well you love others, both grow out of how you love yourself. That is not a slogan. It is mechanics. You receive love at the level of your own self-regard. A person who privately believes he is worth very little will discount every love that arrives. They would stop if they really knew me. This will not last. I have to earn my place here again today. The filter turned inward is still the filter.

Healthy love of yourself is not vanity, and it is not thinking yourself the most important person in the room. It is agreeing with God's valuation of you: a life He made, He loves without condition, and He handed to you to steward. The command Jesus quoted assumes it as the measuring stick: "Love your neighbor as yourself" (Mark 12:31). As yourself. Self-love was never the enemy of loving others. Done right, it is the unit of measure.

When it is healthy, it looks like this. You can receive a compliment or a kindness without deflecting it. You can apologize without collapsing, and be corrected without feeling erased. You can be alone without feeling abandoned. And you can say no without a guilt performance. You cannot pour from an empty cup, and you cannot receive into a cup you believe is worthless.

The other side: do not call poor treatment love

A clear filter cuts both ways. The same honesty that stops you from distorting a tired tone into coldness also stops you from distorting real mistreatment into love. Receiving love well does not mean accepting whatever is handed to you and calling it love. Contempt is not love in a rough dialect. Neither is a chronically broken word, being used, or being diminished in front of others. Naming those things accurately is not scorekeeping. It is the filter working correctly.

You control how you let people treat you. Not what they attempt. What you accept, answer, and stay for. Psychologists Henry Cloud and John Townsend, in their book Boundaries, put it this way: a boundary is the line where you end and the other person begins, and it only becomes real when it carries a consequence. State the standard calmly and early, before resentment does the talking. Attach the consequence and keep it without anger. Then let the consequence do the work.

Peaceful trust and boundaries are not opposites. They are the same settledness facing two directions. A person whose worth is settled by a love that cannot be withdrawn does not have to accept mistreatment to keep people close. There are two errors here, in opposite directions. Error one is reading love as rejection, the dirty filter. Error two is reading mistreatment as love, the filter switched off. The goal is neither suspicion nor gullibility. The goal is a clear lens: receive the real love, refuse the counterfeit.

God's love is in a class of its own

Human love, even the best of it, is responsive. It warms and cools. It gets tired, distracted, wounded, and busy. It can be withdrawn, and sometimes it is. That is not an accusation against the people who love you. It is the condition of every love a human being can offer, including yours.

God's love is a different kind of thing, and the difference has a name: unconditional. Unconditional means the terms are not about you. Human love usually runs on some version of "because." God's love runs before all of that. Paul put it this way: "while we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). The love arrived before the improvement project did. It is not earned by performance, so it cannot be lost by failure. It is not increased by your best week, so it is not decreased by your worst one. And Paul wrote that nothing "will be able to separate us from the love of God" (Romans 8:39).

That is what makes this love so steadying. Every human love can be doubted on a bad day, because humans change and days do. This one cannot be lost on any day, because it was never resting on the day. People love you in the middle of their own lives, with all the weather that brings. God loves you from outside the weather. Anchor to the one that does not move, and you will read the others more generously.

For a Catholic, receiving God's love is not only a mindset. It is a sacrament. At Mass, love is not a feeling you have to manufacture. It is handed to you. "This is my body, given for you." In Communion you do the most Catholic thing there is about love: you come forward and receive it, the way you receive it from the people in your life, except that this Giver never misses. A man who receives at the altar every week is being trained, bodily, in the exact skill this article is about.

Start with one day of noticing

You do not fix a filter all at once. You practice. Set the filter in the morning: name what you are looking for today, the care of the people who love you and God's provision, because the brain finds what it is aimed at. Catch one bid today, and turn toward it on purpose. Translate one act of love that arrived in someone else's dialect, and count it as what it is. Say one act of love out loud, with a specific thank you. And before bed, end the day with the settled sentence: I was loved today, by the people in my life and by God. I noticed. That is enough.

It starts with a man or a woman who is settled about being loved, and who can therefore hold a standard, take a correction, weather a bad quarter, and treat people well, without any of it deciding what they are worth.
